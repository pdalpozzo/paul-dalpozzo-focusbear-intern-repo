***Who are vulnerable populations?***
- Older adults
- People with disabilities
- Low-income households
- Children and adolescents
- Indigenous communities
- Culturally and Linguistically Diverse (CALD) communities
- Remote and rural communities

I just want to touch on why each of these would be considered vulnerable populations.  

**Older Adults**  
It is well know that the elderly struggle to keep up with how fast technology is changing. When making an application for the elderly you will need to consider size of text, visibility of items, and a very simple flow of the application. It is not that they get confused, its more that they are intimidated by learning something complex. This happens in many people at many ages, but the elderly struggle more. Evidence of how elderly struggle is shown in how often they fall victim to scams on the internet.

**People with Disabilities**  
This includes physical, cognitive or sensory impairments. Physical impairments is a fairly simple one to understand when considering we mostly design applications for inputs via keyboard, mouse or touchscreen. Cognitive impairments are also easy to understand in this regard. Depending on the severity of the impairment, you could have people that don't understand how or why things exist, they can struggle to focus on tasks, their memory could be limited, language skills could be limited. This is the target audience of Focus Bear; mainly Autistic and ADHD disabilities. These disabilities also fall into the sensory impairment category. Their minds can be 'overloaded' if too much is presented to them at once.

**Low-income Households**  
This can include homeless people. This population is most affected by obtaining devices. Affordability plays a massive part.

**Children and Adolescents**  
This population is affected by their ability to judge content. Some countries (like Australia) also have bans for internet access for people under a certain age. 

**Indigenous Communities**  
These communities struggle with adapting to society standards. This is mostly due to how they are treated by society. They struggle to let go of the ways they were taught while growing up and adapting to a new lifestyle where things are more accessible. I have heard stories of this from my wife. She works for an Aboriginal company that helps members of their community get their life back on track when affected by drugs or alcohol.

**Culturally and Linguistically Diverse (CALD) Communities**  
This population is vulnerable due to language. When applications or websites are in a language you do not understand, it is easy to struggle using them.

**Remote and Rural Communities**  
This population seems like they are affected by infrastructure and accessibility issues. 

### What ethical considerations are important when designing or working with neurodivergent individuals?
- Respecting Natural Variation - understand that people will see and interpret things differently, and design choices should consider and accommodate these differences.
- Communication - provide clear (jargon-free) explanations, use visual aids and offer multi-modal formats (text, audio, video).
- Avoid Coercion - avoid the user feeling like they are pressured to participate. 
- Active Participation - gather feedback from the people you are designing for.
- Prevent Sensory Overload - avoid auto-playing media, flashing animations, unpredictable pop-ups, and cluttered layouts.
- Predictability and Control - provide a high level of control for the user to customize their experience using the application.
- Cognitive Load Reduction - user clear language and avoid 'walls of text' and chances of misinterpretation.
- Algorithmic Bias - avoid punishing neurodivergent individuals for unique traits that don't fall into the 'typical'.
- Protect Behavioral Data - keep their data private and do not use it to exploit their vulnerabilities.
- Avoid Harmful Tropes - avoid framing the neurodivergent traits as a tragedy or burden to themselves or others

A side note:
1. Not all feedback should be blindly implemented. Users have an idea of what they want or expect, but that does not mean they are able to communicate it correctly. It is important to understand the feedback and what it is trying to suggest.
2. Data gathering is useful but that data can also become harmful to the user if it gets into the wrong hands. A good idea is to allow users to participate with small data collection periods that automatically stop after the set time frame. Keep the data anonymous. Sometime like eye tracking would be a massive help for the UI/UX team, but it needs to be handled correctly and with respect to the user. And consent MUST be given.

### How can you make interactions and content more accessible for people with ADHD or Autism?
The biggest concern here is to allow the user to customize their experience. It is impossible to know/predict every users preferred way of using the application, so allow them to choose. Use simple and direct wording. Avoid putting too many visuals on the screen at once. Make them feel like they are adapting the application to their way instead of them feeling pressure to interact with the application in the way a designer is forcing them.

### How can we support neurodivergent team members in a professional setting?  
It is important to at least understand that everyone works differently. It might not be possible to guess how each person will want to interact or be approached, and it might even be the exact opposite of how you might want to do things. But the key is to ask, be respectful and come to a common understanding where both parties are happy to participate. All great projects need a team and we cannot do our best work if we are always at odds with each other.

### Practice writing a clear, patient, and supportive response to a hypothetical user struggling with executive functioning.
Below is how I think I would respond. I would try to make them feel safe by letting them know this is normal and natural and that I understand. I would use language like 'us' and 'our' to show that we are in this together. I would suggest a breathing exercise to help calm them. Then I would offer a few ideas on how to take the next step.  I would point them to solutions and features that exist in the Focus Bear app. 

```
Hello <users name>, 

I understand that you are feeling overwhelmed; this is natural and completely fine. Let's try some things to help you get back on track.  

Our first step is to reduce the feeling of being overwhelmed. Let's combat that by taking some deep breathes. 2 seconds in, 2 seconds hold, 2 seconds out, 2 seconds hold, and repeat until you feel like you can continue.  
Our next step is to try one of the following; choose whichever suits you best. You could do them all if you want. Just remember, there is no wrong way and no rush. 

    1. Brain Dump - Open up the Notes tab on the Focus Bear app and use the Brain Dump field to write down in point form everything on your mind. It does not matter about order of things. Just let it out.

    2. A Small Decision - Pick ONE thing you want to do. Go to the Focus Bear Todo List tab and add it as a new task. Give it a name. Skip down to the description field and write down what you want to do to achieve this task. If you like, you can break down the task into smaller subtasks that can be ticked off as you complete them. Click Save. If you like, you can start a focus session to start working on that task.

    3. If you just need some quiet time to 'reset', take 5 minutes to reduce your load. You can do this by sitting facing a wall or staring out the window or by watching a plant. How you do it and the duration is up to you. 
```
