## Research & Learn
### What are examples of professional vs. unprofessional behaviour in a casual work environment? (Hint: Think about communication, meeting etiquette, and teamwork.)
***Professional Behaviour***
- communicating respectfully
- meeting deadlines
- asking for help when needed - we all need help
- being aware of personal space
- give updates on workload and progress
- include others
- resolve issues quickly before it affects your work and spreads to others - do this in a respectful and private manner
- manners - please, thank you, you're welcome, good morning, goodbye, sorry, etc. They go a long way.

***Unprofessional Behaviour***
- shouting - being loud and disruptive
- laughing at others
- name calling
- interrupting a meeting - whispering to others, talking over the current speaker, sleeping, sidetracking the conversation
- never meeting deadlines
- touching others - some coworkers might be neurodivergent and see contact as an unwanted action

### What does respectful communication look like in remote teams? (e.g., tone in messages, handling disagreements, responding to feedback)
- Tone in messages can be hard to read. You want to avoid using strong negative words like 'slack', 'wrong', 'stupid'. These will set a negative tone for the rest of the message.  
- Try to read the message without pushing any emotions on to them. If you are upset that you cannot get some code working and read a message, try not to impose that upset tone onto the message.  
- Definitely don't reply with an angry emotional state. If a message upset you and you think it is attacking you, ask the person to clarify what they mean. Don't just assume the worst and retaliate with an angry tone. That would escalate the situation.
- If you believe a person is being mean/disrespectful to you, you should follow the policies in place to report and handle the issue. Try to resolve it in private, but report it to a manager if it doesn't get resolved.
- It's ok to speak your mind and disagree with something, but provide reasons and evidence as to why. Don't simply say that 'its wrong' or 'its dumb'. Also, be respectful
- Listen to others. This is probably the biggest one. People rarely listen to each other. Try to understand the message that the other person is trying to get across, not just hear the words they speak. Ask questions if something is unclear.
- If misunderstandings are happening often with a particular person, try a different approach to communicating with them. If you find they get upset that you go to their desk to speak to them, try a text message. If you find that they ignore your messages, maybe organize a short meeting.

### How can you give and receive feedback professionally? (e.g., focus on actions, not people; assume good intent; ask for clarification instead of reacting defensively)
Like I mention above, try to remove the emotion from the feedback. Try to understand what the feedback is saying. Don't respond back in anger. Remember that we are a team and trying to provide a good product. Remember that some coworkers might have communication traits that they cannot control (like ticks or triggers).  
When providing feedback, write it down and read over it a few times. Try to remove emotions from it. Add as much detail as you can so the feedback can be reproduced. Maybe suggest a solution or the include the result you expected.

## Reflection
### Have you ever experienced or witnessed great teamwork and professionalism? What made it effective?
The problem with this question is that when things go well, it is hard to notice. Lots of people only notice when things are more difficult than they should be; I am one of those people.  
In my concrete career there were days where things ran smoothly. Coworkers would be working the concrete at a good speed. The team had extra time to have a chat and a laugh because the concrete was under control and no one person was working harder than the others. These were the days when it felt great to be a concreter and part of a team that worked well.  
But, it didn't happen every day. Maybe someone had an argument with their wife before coming to work and is a bit more distracted than usual. They work a bit slower and do 80% of their job correctly. Then this ripples through the whole team. Then it amplifies when the concrete starts going off (a term used when the concrete is drying and the team work is falling behind).  

It is important to keep your cool, ask for help, communicate about the status of things. Hopefully the team pulls together to get the job done.

### What steps can you take to make sure your communication is clear, respectful, and inclusive?
In an office environment things are a bit more chill. There is no concrete determining when the deadline is. So, I tend to take my time when communicating. 
- If it's via a message, read over it, don't repeat myself, remove emotions, explain myself clearly, avoid ambiguous terms/phases. 
- If it's face to face, make sure I keep things brief, direct and avoid chit-chat. I can always say that I have a more detailed document for them instead of spending more time face to face.
- If its in a meeting, focus on the objective of the meeting. Have an agenda, work out the issues, create a document on the minutes to send to the participants afterward.
- If someone is quiet in a meeting, include them by asking if they have any questions or anything they want to report on.

### How can you help create a positive, fun, and professional work environment?
In my experience, the team is happy when they feel like everyone is pulling their weight and contributing to the goal. It is much harder to track when everyone is at a computer, but that is why updating people on progress is good and even asking questions when you are stuck. People will see that you are trying to work through your issues and will be more willing to help. This also builds up a rapport with others and will encourage them to include you in things like going to lunch.

### How can you prepare effectively for meetings with supervisors and colleagues?
- Before the meeting, go over the task/issues/goals that will be discussed during the meeting.
- Have some questions lined up. Don't be scared to ask them. They might present a perspective that has not been thought of yet.
- Avoid chit-chat. Keep the meetings short by keeping on topic.
- Take notes. Bring a notepad and pen to record note during the meeting. There are also apps that you can use to record the meeting that will provide a transcript for you. Just be sure to get consent to record others.
- Leave the meeting understanding how to progress. Make sure you reach the goal of your meeting, understand what actions are required of you, maybe you are waiting on someone to finish their work for you to continue, don't leave the meeting and still be confused.

### What would it look like to be proactive during your internship?
- Give regular updates on progress. A daily update is enough, maybe a line or two about the task you are on, if you are making progress, and any blockers you encountered or need to rectify.
- Taking notes in meetings. 
- Don't sit idle. If you are not sure about what you should be doing, explore the application, ask a coworker, or even bring an idea of what you think you should be doing to a supervisor to get it approved.
- Learn from your feedback. Don't keep making the same mistake and assuming someone will catch them. Fix them proactively.

### How might you follow up with colleagues or supervisors if you need information/action from them? How would you escalate it further if you don't get a response and when would you escalate?
- First, I would make sure I know what I am waiting for and know that there is no way forward without it.
- I would contact the colleague via direct message (using the app they check most often; found in their User Manual). I would make sure to contact them before a break (lunch or meeting). I like to do this so that they see the notification when they get back to their desk. 
- Depending on urgency of the action/information, I would allow some time for the colleague to respond but follow up if no response is given. For something urgent (needed within a couple of hours), I would send a message, wait about 20 minutes, approach them face to face, alert them that I have tried to contact them and it is urgent, then wait for them to respond.
- If no response is given, I would ask them if they don't mind me finding the information myself and ask them to point me in the direction to find it. 
- If this turns into a real blocker for me I will offer my help to the colleague. Maybe the task I am waiting for them to complete is large and they need help. It would speed up the whole process if I help instead of constantly hound them for work. If they let me help, I would alert any managers or supervisors that I am doing so.
- If they don't want me to help i will ask for a timeline of when to expect things. I would continue on with other work while I wait. I will alert any managers or supervisors that I am waiting for that action/information and that the colleague has given me a rough timeline of when to expect it, and let them know what I will be working on in the meantime.

### What are some things you won't do?
- I won't call out a person. I understand that people have ups and downs. I get it. I will always try to offer my time to help. But I will also alert the higher ups on my progress if what I am doing is different to what they expect. I don't do this to get others in trouble, I do it so that the communication is flowing and people are aware of the time it takes to do things.
- I won't push or badger someone that I am waiting on.
- I won't be sitting idle waiting for someone to tell me what to do next. I get bored.
- I won't exclude others.
- I won't waste others' time.
- I won't provide vague feedback.
